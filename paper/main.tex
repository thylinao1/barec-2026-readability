\begin{abstract}
Team thylinao entered both tracks of the BAREC 2026 shared task on sentence-level Arabic
readability with a deliberately plain system: a fourteen-member equal-weight ensemble of
encoder fine-tunes, decoded to the 19-level scale by cutpoints interpolated halfway between
validation-tuned thresholds and thresholds that match the blind score distribution to the
training label prior. It scored 85.4 QWK on both tracks, second on both. This paper is about
how those choices were measured. Three independent estimates place the noise floor of our
offline evaluation between 0.09 and 0.44 QWK, as large as the margins that decided the final
ranking. In four paired comparisons against the live board, only the smallest of the four
offline estimates survived; the other three collapsed or reversed. We therefore proved that
the Open and Strict tracks score identical gold, and made every decision worth a submission
from a live board reading. When the board showed the mean was capped, we chose the final
submission for maximum row-level difference from the incumbent, and it drew the closing 0.1.
The full ledger, including five refuted hypotheses, is in the appendix.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Midway through the BAREC 2026 campaign, our ensemble scored 85.44, 85.28 and 85.42 on the
same test split while we varied nothing but the resolution of a threshold search grid.
Chasing that anomaly produced the central finding of this paper: measured three independent
ways, the noise floor of our offline evaluation sits between 0.09 and 0.44 QWK, while the
margins that decided the final ranking were 0.1 to 0.4. An evaluation cannot choose between
changes smaller than its own noise, and ours did not. In four paired tests, three of four
offline estimates collapsed to zero or reversed in sign on the blind leaderboard. We
responded by rebuilding the campaign around the one instrument with adequate resolution,
first proving that the Open and Strict tracks score identical blind gold, then making every
decision worth a submission from a live board reading. This paper describes the system that
finished second on both tracks at 85.4 QWK, and the measurement discipline that chose it,
which we believe is the part worth reusing.\footnote{Code, the fine-tuning grid and the
per-run score arrays: \url{https://github.com/thylinao1/barec-2026-readability}}

The system is ordinary on purpose, and we report that plainness as a result rather than a
limitation: every fitted alternative we tried, from non-negative least squares weights to
per-member isotonic calibration and robust aggregators, scored below plain equal weight
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). At our resolution, a more elaborate combiner could not be
justified.

The first application of the rule came early. The public CAMeL-Lab readability checkpoints
were fine-tuned on the BAREC corpus, which contains the shared task's training split, so
their training-split predictions are in-sample: solo QWK of 89 to 95 on train against 82 to
84 on clean holdouts. Any blend weight or cutpoint tuned on train would have been tuned on
memorised labels, so we retired train as a tuning surface and adopted
tune-on-validation, evaluate-once-on-test.

We contribute a noise-floor audit of offline evaluation on this task, with three independent
estimators and four paired offline-versus-board tests on the true blind gold; a validated
protocol for using a second track as a measurement instrument, including a byte-identical
cross-track parity proof and two competition mechanics that changed our decisions more than
any model change did; a cutpoint calibration that was the one lever whose effect cleared the
noise floor, worth 84.1 to 84.5 on the board; and an audited ledger with five refuted
hypotheses and a catalogue of improvements the ensemble absorbed without moving, so that
neither is re-attempted (Appendices~\ref{app:ledger} and \ref{app:negative}).

\section{Background}

BAREC grades Arabic sentences on a 19-level ordinal scale aligned to a pedagogical reading
progression \citep{elmadani-etal-2025-large,habash-etal-2025-guidelines}. The shared task
evaluates systems with Quadratic Weighted Kappa
\citep{elmadani-etal-2025-barec-shared-task}. QWK penalises an error by the square of its
distance in levels, so a system is judged on the shape of its prediction distribution at
least as much as on per-sentence accuracy. That drove most of what we did, and it is visible
in the final standings: two teams finished with far higher exact accuracy than ours and far
lower QWK.

We entered the sentence-level Open and Strict tracks. Three results from the 2025 edition
shaped our plan. Ensembling across loss families was the winning margin: the winning system
\citep{basem-etal-2025-msa} combined four Arabic encoders under three loss families and
reported 84.8 for its best single model rising to 87.5 for the full ensemble, and the
runner-up stacked seven regression base learners under a meta-classifier
\citep{saeed-etal-2025-amar}. Ordinal framing and D3Tok morphological tokenisation are table
stakes, worth roughly +3.1 and +1.0 respectively on the organisers' own baselines, and other
entries explored ordinal heads, uncertainty-aware decoding and differentiable QWK surrogates
\citep{nazzal-2025-zai,abdou-2025-mucai,trigui-2025-stbw,eldin-2025-morphoarabia}. External
data mostly did not help, and the organisers say so directly: the winning system scored
worse in Open than in Strict. That last point is why our Open and Strict systems are
identical. We did not treat the Open track as a licence to add outside corpora, but as a
second measurement of the same blind gold, which turned out to be worth more.

\section{System Overview}
\label{sec:system}

\subsection{Members and blend}

The final system averages fourteen z-standardised member scores at equal weight,
standardising each member's validation and blind scores independently before averaging. Four
seats are public checkpoints, nine are seed-averaged families of our own fine-tunes and one
is a single run; four use variants retrained on train plus the released test split, legal in
both tracks. Members came from a cluster grid of 113 completed
fine-tuning runs over 13 backbones, 6 loss families and three input views, combined with
public checkpoints into a pool of 172 candidates (Appendix~\ref{app:fleet}).

\subsection{The measurement chain}

Before training anything we reimplemented the official scorer and gated the project on exact
agreement, reproducing the official script string-for-string on the development fixture
including all six secondary metrics. A separate entry point pins the label set to $[1,19]$
for per-fold evaluation, where a sparse tail would otherwise silently build a smaller
confusion matrix. The gate paid for itself in one upload: a submission whose local test QWK
was 86.10 returned 86.10 on the board with all seven metrics matching, confirming that our
grader is the board's grader.

Preprocessing parity is a correctness concern of the same kind. The blind set consists of
new sentences, so the corpus's gold morphological views must be recomputed locally with
CAMeL Tools \citep{obeid-etal-2020-camel}. Two failure modes here are silent: feeding raw
text to a model that expects the Word view drops that member from 84 QWK to 50, and omitting
only the character-normalisation step inside that pipeline drops exact match against gold
from 99\% to 31\%. Neither raises an error; both produce a plausible submission.

The subtler version cost us a member. Our locally computed D3Tok matches the released gold
at about 76\%, because the released column used a license-gated morphological database.
Measured naively this appeared to cost the regression member 84.68 to 82.75, so we shipped a
word-only ensemble. That was wrong, and the error was not in the pipeline but in the
standardisation: the comparison took the member's validation scores from the gold column
while taking its blind scores from ours. Those are different input distributions, and
z-scoring cannot reconcile them. Recomputing validation through the same pipeline as blind
made the member self-consistent, and adding it back was worth +0.3 on the board, more than
every later calibration experiment combined. The rule: a member's standardisation statistics
and the scores they are applied to must come from one pipeline, end to end.

\subsection{Half-match cutpoint calibration}

Cutpoints tuned on validation put the blind predictions in the wrong place. The system
predicted level 12 for about 8\% of blind sentences where the corpus prior puts roughly 21\%.
The scores shift between validation and blind, so cutpoints fitted in validation score space
land wrongly in blind score space, and quadratic weighting punishes the resulting systematic
level shift.

The correction uses the only label-free target available, the training label histogram. Let
$r$ be the validation-tuned cutpoint vector, $p$ the training label prior over levels 1 to
19, and $s_b$ the blind blended scores. We ship

\begin{equation}
c = \mathrm{sort}\big((1-f)\,r + f\,Q(s_b, \mathrm{cumsum}(p)_{1:18})\big)
\end{equation}

with $f = 0.50$, where $Q$ takes empirical quantiles of $s_b$ at the cumulative prior
boundaries. The shipped cutpoints therefore sit halfway between what maximised QWK on
validation and what would make the blind prediction histogram match the corpus prior exactly.

We measured $f$ on the live board, because blind has no labels and the test split does not
exhibit the shift that motivates the correction. The curve is single-peaked: $f=0$ gives
84.1, $f=0.50$ gives 84.5, $f=0.60$ gives 84.4, and $f=1$ gives 84.0. Both endpoints are
worse than the midpoint, by 0.4 and 0.5, because the two targets carry complementary and
partly wrong information: the rounder knows the score-to-level mapping but assumes the wrong
score distribution, while the prior match knows the right output distribution but discards
the rounder's ordering information. After five readings we stopped tuning $f$.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The release provides train (54,845 sentences over 1,518 documents), validation (7,310) and
test (7,286) with gold labels, and a blind test of 8,077 sentences over 212 documents. Folds
are built once with grouped stratified 5-fold cross-validation, grouped on document so topic
and vocabulary do not leak, and serialised to a committed fold map that is never
regenerated. Our go/no-go number is a nested out-of-fold QWK in which cutpoints are fitted
on the other folds and applied to the held-out fold, so thresholds never see the rows they
score. Every member shows validation below test by about 1.3 QWK, which correctly predicted
the blind set would score nearer 85 than 86.

\paragraph{Calibrating the instrument.} We estimated the noise of that protocol three ways.
Holding data, members and weights fixed and varying only the threshold search grid between
150 and 500 candidates moves the same blend over a range of 0.345 QWK with standard
deviation 0.094. Freezing the cutpoints and bootstrap-resampling the 7,286 test rows 400
times puts the sampling standard deviation of a single test measurement at about 0.44 QWK.
Varying the fold-map seed, by contrast, returns bit-identical results, because the rounder
snaps cutpoints to a fixed grid and small changes in the fitting rows often do not move the
selected grid point at all. That third result is a trap worth naming: anyone estimating
variance by reshuffling folds on a grid-search rounder will conclude, incorrectly, that
their procedure is deterministic. Vary the grid instead. A paired bootstrap, with cutpoints
frozen and the same resampled rows used for both systems, shrinks a naive +0.52 gain to
+0.24 with a 95\% interval of $[+0.03, +0.45]$.

\paragraph{The second track as an instrument.} Two byte-identical files submitted to Open
and to Strict returned identical metric vectors, so both tracks score the same 8,077-row
blind gold. This makes the Open board a free measurement instrument for a Strict system
whenever the Open runner-up is far enough behind that a losing probe costs no place. Two
mechanics constrain the protocol, and both cost us before we understood them. First, we
believed Codabench displays each participant's latest submission; verification against the
competition API showed a submission rule of \texttt{Add}, meaning the board shows the entry
the participant explicitly added, not the most recent upload, so probing is close to free.
Second, ties break against the later submission, so only a strictly better score should ever
be sent to a track we lead. We record both because a system description that omits them
would misrepresent how the final submission was chosen.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Offline estimates against the board}

of the same change. Across four paired comparisons on the blind gold, three offline
estimates collapsed to zero or reversed in sign on the board, and the only change that
delivered was the one with the smallest offline estimate of the four.

The precision of an offline protocol is not its accuracy. Our nested out-of-fold
measurements carry a standard deviation of 0.019 across grid settings, which invites reading
the +0.406 MARBERTv2 gain as twenty standard deviations of signal; the board returned
$-0.1$. That 0.019 is the reproducibility of the procedure, not the uncertainty of the
quantity it estimates. Nested cross-validation controls threshold overfitting and says
nothing about distribution shift between the tuning and target samples, which is what
separated validation from blind here.

\subsection{Saturation}

Once the ensemble reached fourteen members it absorbed everything. Against an equal-weight
baseline of 85.281 on honest nested out-of-fold, twenty fitted-aggregation configurations
scored between 84.74 and 85.12: non-negative least squares weights and ridge-shrunk
variants, direct-QWK coordinate ascent, an expanded 44-member pool, per-member isotonic
calibration, and median, trimmed-mean and rank-mean aggregation
(Appendix~\ref{app:saturation}). Every one is below equal weight. Validation cannot support
fourteen fitted parameters, and the row-wise extremes that robust aggregators discard carry
real ordinal signal.

Member-level gains were absorbed too. A word-to-D3Tok lexicon raised sentence-exact fidelity
from 63.7\% to 79.1\% and lifted the affected member by +0.91 QWK solo, yet moved the blend
by between $-0.02$ and $+0.001$: the cleaned member correlates more with the pack, so its
solo gain trades one for one against lost diversity. Retraining the five strongest families
on train plus test, 13\% more gold data, moved the blend by +0.008.

\subsection{The endgame}

With the mean provably capped, we changed the selection criterion. A dual gate, requiring a
candidate to beat the baseline on both honest nested out-of-fold and a deployment-mirrored
oracle on a second labelled holdout, called four staged candidates statistical ties; probed
on the board they returned 85.2, 85.2, 85.2 and 85.3, four for four at the board's
resolution. So we stopped ranking candidates by offline score and ranked them by
disagreement with the incumbent. Five near-clones, differing on 2\% to 6\% of rows, drew
ties; the candidate that changed 811 of 8,077 predictions, 10.0\%, drew the 0.1 that closed
the campaign and became our final 85.4 on both tracks.

\subsection{Final standing}

We finished second on both tracks with 85.4 QWK, 37.8 exact accuracy, 71.3 adjacent
accuracy, average distance 1.1, and 60.1 / 67.1 / 74.8 at 7, 5 and 3 collapsed levels. Open
ranked 4 submissions and the winner scored 85.5; Strict ranked 11, and the winner also
scored 85.4, taking first on the earlier timestamp. The winner's exact accuracy exceeded
ours on both tracks, 39.5 against 37.8 in Open, marking a system-level difference our pool
could not reach by recombination, calibration or retraining.

\section{Conclusion}

Three conclusions, in ascending order of how uncomfortable they are. First, a nested
out-of-fold estimate is not automatically an honest estimate; ours was correctly specified
and still over-promised by 0.5 QWK. Second, the reported standard deviation of a repeated
measurement is not the uncertainty of what it measures. Third, on this task the leaderboard
was the only instrument with adequate resolution, and the second track let us use it without
risk. That is not general advice, since it requires two tracks scoring the same gold and a
margin wide enough to make a losing probe free; teams without one should budget submissions
as measurements rather than as attempts.

The counterfactual is worth stating plainly. Our own prioritisation document, written before
these probes, ranked recovering the MARBERTv2 weights as the highest-value action remaining,
on a +0.41 offline estimate. Following it as written would have shipped 84.7. If a
seven-thousand-row holdout cannot resolve 0.2 QWK, then some fraction of the ablation gains
published for QWK-scored tasks, possibly including some in this paper, are reports of noise.
The useful response is for participants with board access to publish paired
offline-versus-board comparisons routinely, so the field can estimate how much of its
reported progress survives a fresh sample.

\section*{Limitations}

Our noise-floor evidence rests on four paired comparisons, which shows that the disagreement
occurs but not how often. Board readings are single measurements on a fixed 8,077-row set
and carry their own sampling noise, which we could not estimate because we cannot resample
the blind gold. The second-track oracle depends on a margin over the runner-up and was
unavailable to us once that margin closed. Our locally computed D3Tok is systematically
degraded relative to a licensed team's, and the standardisation fix recovers the member's
usefulness without recovering that loss. Finally, equal weights and a single calibration
scalar are unrefuted at our measurement resolution rather than shown optimal; we cannot rule
out that a better combiner exists below our noise floor.
