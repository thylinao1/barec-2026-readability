\begin{abstract}
Team thylinao entered both tracks of the BAREC 2026 shared task on sentence-level Arabic
readability with a deliberately plain system: a fourteen-member equal-weight ensemble of
encoder fine-tunes, decoded to the 19-level scale by cutpoints interpolated halfway between
validation-tuned thresholds and thresholds that match the blind score distribution to the
training label prior. It scored 85.3 QWK on both tracks, second on both. This paper is about
how those choices were measured. Three independent estimates place the noise floor of our
offline evaluation between 0.09 and 0.44 QWK, as large as the margins that decided the final
ranking. In three paired comparisons against the live board, only the smallest of the three
offline estimates survived; the other two reversed in sign. We therefore proved that the Open
and Strict tracks score identical gold, and made every decision worth a submission from a live
board reading. Once the board showed the mean was capped, every further candidate came back a
tie at the board's resolution. The full ledger, including four refuted hypotheses and one
withdrawn submission, is in the appendix.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Midway through the BAREC 2026 campaign, our ensemble scored 85.44, 85.28 and 85.42 on the
same test split while we varied nothing but the resolution of a threshold search grid.
Chasing that anomaly produced the central finding of this paper: measured three independent
ways, the noise floor of our offline evaluation sits between 0.09 and 0.44 QWK, while the
margins that decided our two placings were 0.1 and 0.2 QWK. An evaluation cannot choose between
changes smaller than its own noise, and ours did not. In three paired tests, two of three
offline estimates reversed in sign on the blind leaderboard. We
responded by rebuilding the campaign around the one instrument with adequate resolution,
first proving that the Open and Strict tracks score identical blind gold, then making every
decision worth a submission from a live board reading. This paper describes the system that
finished second on both tracks at 85.3 QWK, and the measurement discipline that chose it,
which we believe is the part worth reusing.\footnote{Code, the fine-tuning grid and the
per-run score arrays: \url{https://github.com/thylinao1/barec-2026-readability}}

The system is ordinary on purpose, and we report that plainness as a result: every fitted
alternative we tried scored below plain equal weight (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

The first application of the rule came early. The public CAMeL-Lab readability checkpoints
were fine-tuned on the BAREC corpus, which contains the shared task's training split, so
their training-split predictions are in-sample: solo QWK of 89 to 95 on train against 82 to
84 on clean holdouts. Any blend weight or cutpoint tuned on train would have been tuned on
memorised labels, so we retired train as a tuning surface and adopted
tune-on-validation, evaluate-once-on-test.

We contribute a noise-floor audit of offline evaluation on this task, with three independent
estimators and three paired offline-versus-board tests on the true blind gold; a protocol for
using a second track as a measurement instrument, including a cross-track parity proof and two
competition mechanics that changed our decisions more than any model change did; a cutpoint
calibration that was the one lever clearing the noise floor, worth 84.1 to 84.5 on the board;
and an audited ledger of four refuted hypotheses and the improvements the ensemble absorbed
without moving (Appendices~\ref{app:ledger} and \ref{app:negative}).

\section{Background}

BAREC grades Arabic sentences on a 19-level ordinal scale aligned to a pedagogical reading
progression \citep{elmadani-etal-2025-large,habash-etal-2025-guidelines}. The shared task
evaluates systems with Quadratic Weighted Kappa
\citep{elmadani-etal-2026-barec-shared-task}. QWK penalises an error by the square of its
distance in levels, so a system is judged on the shape of its prediction distribution at
least as much as on per-sentence accuracy. That drove most of what we did, and it is visible
in the final standings: two teams finished with far higher exact accuracy than ours and far
lower QWK.

We entered the sentence-level Open and Strict tracks. Three results from the 2025 edition
shaped our plan. Ensembling across loss families was the winning margin: the winner
\citep{basem-etal-2025-msa} combined four encoders under three loss families and reported 84.8
for its best single model rising to 87.5 for the ensemble, and the runner-up stacked seven
regression base learners under a meta-classifier \citep{saeed-etal-2025-amar}. Ordinal framing and D3Tok morphological tokenisation are table
stakes, worth roughly +3.1 and +1.0 on the organisers' own baselines, and other entries
explored ordinal heads, uncertainty-aware decoding and QWK surrogates
\citep{nazzal-2025-zai,abdou-2025-mucai,trigui-2025-stbw,eldin-2025-morphoarabia}. External
data mostly did not help, and the organisers say so directly
\citep{elmadani-etal-2025-barec-shared-task}: the winning system scored worse in Open than in
Strict. That is why our Open and Strict systems are identical. We treated the Open track not as
a licence to add outside corpora but as a second measurement of the same blind gold, which
turned out to be worth more.

\section{System Overview}
\label{sec:system}

\subsection{Members and blend}

The final system averages fourteen z-standardised member scores at equal weight,
standardising each member's validation and blind scores independently before averaging. Four
seats are public BAREC fine-tunes released by others, nine are seed-averaged families of our
own fine-tunes and one is a single run. Ours use the BAREC training split alone
(Section~\ref{sec:correction}). Members came from a cluster grid of 112 completed
fine-tuning runs over 13 backbones, 6 loss families and three input views, combined with
public checkpoints into a pool of 172 candidates (Appendix~\ref{app:fleet}).

\subsection{The measurement chain}

Before training anything we reimplemented the official scorer and gated the project on exact
agreement, reproducing the official script string-for-string on the development fixture. A
separate entry point pins the label set to $[1,19]$ for per-fold evaluation, where a sparse
tail would otherwise silently build a smaller confusion matrix. The gate paid for itself in one
upload: a submission whose local test QWK was 86.10 returned 86.10 on the development-phase
board, which scores that released test split, all seven metrics matching, confirming that our
grader is the board's grader.

Preprocessing parity is the same kind of concern. The blind set is new sentences, so the
corpus's gold morphological views must be recomputed locally with
CAMeL Tools \citep{obeid-etal-2020-camel}. Two failure modes here are silent: feeding raw
text to a model that expects the Word view drops that member from 84 QWK to 50, and omitting
only the character-normalisation step inside that pipeline drops exact match against gold
from 99\% to 31\%. Neither raises an error; both produce a plausible submission.

The subtler version cost us a member. Our locally computed D3Tok matches the released gold
at about 76\%, because the released column used a licence-gated morphological database.
Measured naively this appeared to drop the regression member from 84.68 to 82.75, so we
shipped a word-only ensemble. That was wrong, and the error was in the standardisation, not the
pipeline: the comparison took the member's validation scores from the gold column while taking
its blind scores from ours, and z-scoring cannot reconcile two input distributions. Recomputing
validation through the same pipeline as blind made the member self-consistent, and adding it
back was worth +0.3 on the board, more than every later calibration experiment combined. A
member's standardisation statistics and the scores they are applied to must come from one
pipeline, end to end.

\subsection{Half-match cutpoint calibration}

Cutpoints tuned on validation put the blind predictions in the wrong place. The system
predicted level 12 for about 8\% of blind sentences where the corpus prior puts roughly 21\%.
The scores shift between validation and blind, so cutpoints fitted in validation score space
land wrongly in blind score space, and quadratic weighting punishes the resulting systematic
level shift.

The correction uses the only label-free target available, the training label histogram. Let
$r$ be the validation-tuned cutpoint vector, $p$ the training label prior over levels 1 to
19, and $s_b$ the blind blended scores. We ship

\begin{equation}
c = \mathrm{sort}\big((1-f)\,r + f\,Q(s_b, \mathrm{cumsum}(p)_{1:18})\big)
\end{equation}
with $f = 0.50$, where $Q$ takes empirical quantiles of $s_b$ at the cumulative prior
boundaries. The shipped cutpoints sit halfway between what maximised QWK on validation and
what would make the blind prediction histogram match the corpus prior.

We measured $f$ on the live board, because blind has no labels and the test split does not
exhibit the shift that motivates the correction. The curve is single-peaked: $f=0$ gives
84.1, $f=0.50$ gives 84.5, $f=0.60$ gives 84.4, and $f=1$ gives 84.0. Both endpoints are
worse than the midpoint, by 0.4 and 0.5, because the two targets carry complementary and
partly wrong information: the rounder knows the score-to-level mapping but assumes the wrong
score distribution, while the prior match knows the right output distribution but discards
the rounder's ordering information. After five readings we stopped tuning $f$.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The release provides train (54,845 sentences over 1,518 documents), validation (7,310) and
test (7,286) with gold labels, and a blind test of 8,077 sentences over 212 documents. Folds
are built once with grouped stratified 5-fold cross-validation, grouped on document so topic
and vocabulary do not leak, and serialised to a committed fold map that is never
regenerated. Our go/no-go number is a nested out-of-fold QWK in which cutpoints are fitted
on the other folds and applied to the held-out fold, so thresholds never see the rows they
score. Every member shows validation below test by about 1.3 QWK, which correctly predicted
the blind set would score nearer 85 than 86.

\paragraph{Calibrating the instrument.} We estimated the noise of that protocol three ways.
Holding data, members and weights fixed and varying only the threshold search grid between
150 and 500 candidates moves the same blend over a range of 0.345 QWK with standard
deviation 0.094. Freezing the cutpoints and bootstrap-resampling the 7,286 test rows 400
times puts the sampling standard deviation of a single test measurement at about 0.44 QWK.
Varying the fold-map seed, by contrast, returns bit-identical results, because the rounder
snaps cutpoints to a fixed grid and small changes in the fitting rows often do not move the
selected grid point. That third result is a trap worth naming: anyone estimating variance by
reshuffling folds on a grid-search rounder will conclude, incorrectly, that their procedure is
deterministic. Vary the grid instead. A paired bootstrap shrinks a naive +0.52 gain, from
adding MARBERTv2 to the three-member word-only blend under tuned weights, to +0.24 with a
95\% interval of $[+0.03, +0.45]$.

\paragraph{The second track as an instrument.} Two byte-identical files submitted to Open
and to Strict returned identical metric vectors, so both tracks score the same 8,077-row
blind gold. This makes the Open board a free measurement instrument for a Strict system
whenever the Open runner-up is far enough behind that a losing probe costs no place. Two
mechanics constrain the protocol, and both cost us before we understood them. First, we
believed Codabench displays each participant's latest submission; the competition API showed a
submission rule of \texttt{Add}, so the board shows the entry the participant explicitly added
and probing is close to free. Second, the board orders equal QWK by exact accuracy, so only a
strictly better score should be sent to a track we lead. We record both because a system
description that omits them would misrepresent how the final submission was chosen.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Offline estimates against the board}

Table~\ref{tab:paired} pairs every offline estimate we acted on with the live board reading of
the same change. Two of the three reversed in sign, and the only change that delivered was the
one with the smallest offline estimate.

The precision of an offline protocol is not its accuracy. Our nested out-of-fold measurements
carry a standard deviation of 0.019 across grid settings, which invites reading the +0.406
MARBERTv2 gain as twenty standard deviations of signal; the board returned $-0.1$. That 0.019
is the reproducibility of the procedure, not the uncertainty of the quantity it estimates.
Nested cross-validation controls threshold overfitting and says nothing about distribution
shift between the tuning and target samples, which is what separated validation from blind.

\subsection{Saturation}

Once the ensemble reached fourteen members it absorbed everything. Against an equal-weight
baseline of 85.281 on honest nested out-of-fold, twenty fitted-aggregation configurations
scored between 84.74 and 85.12: least-squares and ridge-shrunk weights, direct-QWK coordinate
ascent, an expanded 44-member pool, per-member isotonic calibration, and median, trimmed-mean
and rank-mean aggregation (Appendix~\ref{app:saturation}). Every one is below equal weight.
Validation cannot support fourteen fitted parameters, and the row-wise extremes that robust
aggregators discard carry real ordinal signal.

Member-level gains were absorbed too: a word-to-D3Tok lexicon worth +0.91 QWK solo moved the
blend by between $-0.024$ and $+0.001$, because the cleaned member correlates more with the
pack (Appendix~\ref{app:saturation}).

\subsection{The endgame}

With the mean provably capped, we changed the selection criterion. A dual gate, requiring a
candidate to beat the baseline both on honest nested out-of-fold and on a deployment-mirrored
oracle over the released gold test split, called four staged candidates statistical ties; probed
on the board they returned 85.2, 85.2, 85.2 and 85.3, four for four at the board's
resolution. So we stopped ranking candidates by offline score and ranked them by
disagreement with the incumbent. Five near-clones, differing on 2\% to 12\% of rows, drew
ties as well. The board ran out of resolution before our pool ran out of candidates. The test
leg never overturned validation: its one promoted candidate failed the validation leg, and the
board agreed.

\subsection{Final standing}

We finished second on both tracks with 85.3 QWK and 37.7 exact accuracy. Open ranked 4
submissions and the winner scored 85.5; Strict ranked 11 and the winner scored 85.4. The
winner's exact accuracy exceeded ours on both tracks, 39.5 against 37.7 in Open, a difference
our pool could not reach by recombination, calibration or retraining.

\subsection{Correction to the submitted system}
\label{sec:correction}

The entry we first submitted had four of its fourteen seats fine-tuned on the training split
plus the corpus's released gold test split. We disclosed this to the organisers
and in this paper, having read it as permitted; after the phase closed they ruled otherwise and
we withdrew it. Every number here is the same fourteen-seat ensemble with those four seats
replaced by their training-split-only counterparts from the same grid, an entry we also
submitted during the testing phase and which the board scored at 85.3. What the withdrawn
variant bought is measured in Appendix~\ref{app:saturation}: 0.008 offline and 0.1 on the
board, inside every noise estimate in Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\section{Conclusion}

Three conclusions, in ascending order of how uncomfortable they are. First, a nested
out-of-fold estimate is not automatically an honest estimate; ours was correctly specified and
still over-promised by 0.5 QWK. Second, the reported standard deviation of a repeated
measurement is not the uncertainty of what it measures. Third, on this task the leaderboard was
the only instrument with adequate resolution, and the second track let us use it without risk.
That last point is not general advice: it needs two tracks scoring the same gold and a margin
wide enough to make a losing probe free. Teams without one should budget submissions as
measurements rather than as attempts.

The counterfactual is worth stating plainly. Our own prioritisation document, written before
these probes, ranked recovering the MARBERTv2 weights as the highest-value action remaining, on
a +0.41 offline estimate. Following it would have shipped 84.7. If a seven-thousand-row
holdout cannot resolve 0.2 QWK, some fraction of the ablation gains published for QWK-scored
tasks, possibly including some in this paper, are reports of noise. Teams with board access
should publish paired offline-versus-board comparisons.

\section*{Limitations}

Our noise-floor evidence rests on three paired comparisons, enough to show that the disagreement
occurs but not how often. Board readings are single measurements on a fixed 8,077-row set
and carry their own sampling noise, which we could not estimate because we cannot resample
the blind gold. The second-track oracle depends on a margin over the runner-up and was
unavailable to us once that margin closed. Our locally computed D3Tok is systematically
degraded relative to a licensed team's, and the standardisation fix recovers the member's
usefulness without recovering that loss. Finally, equal weights and a single calibration
scalar are unrefuted at our measurement resolution rather than shown optimal; we cannot rule
out that a better combiner exists below our noise floor.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

BAREC frames sentence-level readability along a pedagogical reading progression (\S2), so a
system like ours is most plausibly consumed downstream to grade or select reading material
for learners. We have only evaluated our ensemble on the shared task's own train, validation,
test and blind splits; we do not know how it performs on registers, dialectal admixture, or
genres outside that distribution, and a deployment decision (including or excluding a text
for a given reader) should not extend our reported numbers to a population the corpus does
not represent. The BAREC corpus and annotation guidelines we build on are cited in \S2 and
used under their released research licences; the D3Tok licensing asymmetry discussed above under Limitations is a
data-access fairness concern between shared-task participants, not a claim about harm to end
users of a deployed system.
